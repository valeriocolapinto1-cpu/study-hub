- Single precision (32 bits): **1 sign + 8 exponent + 23 mantissa**. Double (64 bits): 1 + 11 + 52.
- The value is **(−1)<sup>s</sup> × 1.M × 2<sup>E−bias</sup>**: the 1 before the point is **implicit** and not stored, so one bit of precision is gained.
- The **bias** (127 and 1023) makes the exponent an unsigned number: two positive floats are compared by reading their bits as integers.
- Exponent all 0s → zero and denormals. All 1s → infinity (zero mantissa) or **NaN** (non-zero mantissa).

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/da-dove-si-parte">

## Where we start

**What you need to know first:** binary numbering and the idea of positional notation, from the module on [binary numbers](../numeri-binari/).

**What problem it solves.** With two's complement we know how to write integers, but a computer must also handle 3.14 and 6.02 × 10²³. With a fixed number of bits you cannot have everything: either you reserve a fixed number of digits after the point — and then very large and very small numbers do not fit — or you adopt the same idea as **scientific notation**, storing the significant digits and the order of magnitude separately. The second route is that of the IEEE 754 standard, and it is why the point is called “floating”: its position is decided by the exponent.

**New words.** The **mantissa** (or significand) holds the significant digits; the **exponent** says by how many positions to move the point. **Normalising** means writing the number in the form 1.xxx × 2ᵉ, with a single non-zero digit before the point. The **bias** is the constant added to the exponent to make it an unsigned number. **NaN** stands for “not a number” and is the result of undefined operations. A **denormal** number is smaller than the smallest normalised one and gives up the implicit 1 to represent values very close to zero.

</div>

## Why it is needed

With fixed point the number of decimal places is decided once and for all and the range is narrow. Floating point spends its bits on a **binary scientific notation**, `± mantissa × 2^exponent`, obtaining an enormously wider range for the same bits — at the price of **relative** rather than absolute precision.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/singola-precisione-32-bit">

## Single precision (32 bits)

```
| S |   E (8 bits) |        M (23 bits)       |
  1        8                    23
```

- **S**: sign, 0 positive and 1 negative.
- **E**: **biased** exponent with bias **127**: what is stored is `E = real exponent + 127`. It serves to represent negative exponents without a second sign bit, and allows floats to be compared as if they were integers.
- **M**: mantissa, the part after the point. The **hidden bit** “1.” is not stored: when normalising, the digit before the point is always 1, so one bit is gained for free.

Value = `(−1)ˢ · 1.M · 2^(E−127)`

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/come-ci-si-arriva-la-notazione-scientifica">

## How we get there: scientific notation

The idea is not new: it is the one by which you write 6.02 × 10²³ instead of a twenty-four-digit number. The **significant digits** are separated from the **order of magnitude**, and two small numbers are stored instead of one huge one. In binary it becomes:

```
value = ± mantissa × 2^exponent
```

One ambiguity remains: 1.5 × 2³, 0.75 × 2⁴ and 3.0 × 2² are the same number written in three ways. Without a rule, the same quantity would have several encodings — and comparing two numbers would become complicated. So the **normalised** form is imposed: exactly one non-zero digit before the point.

In binary there is only one non-zero digit: 1. So **every** normalised number starts with “1.”. And if it always starts with 1, storing it is a waste: it is taken for granted. It is the **implicit 1**, and it gives a bit of precision for free — 24 bits of effective mantissa out of 23 stored.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/perche-il-bias-e-non-il-complemento-a-2">

## Why the bias, and not two's complement

The exponent must be able to be negative (for numbers smaller than 1). The obvious choice would be two's complement, which we already know. The standard does something else instead: it adds a constant, the **bias**, so that the stored exponent is always positive.

```
single precision:  E_stored = e + 127     (e goes from −126 to +127)
double precision:  E_stored = e + 1023
```

The reason is **comparison**. Lining up sign, exponent and mantissa, and using an increasing unsigned encoding for the exponent, two positive numbers are compared **by reading their bits as if they were integers**: the one with the larger pattern is the larger number. The floating-point comparison circuit becomes the same as the integer one.

With two's complement the property would be lost: a negative exponent would have its top bit at 1 and would come out “greater” than a positive one, reversing the order. The bias is a design decision taken to make the most frequent operation fast.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/arrotondamento">

## Rounding

The mantissa has 23 bits: almost every result must be **rounded**. The standard defines four modes, and the default one is the subtlest:

- **To nearest, ties to even**: the nearest representable value is chosen; at an exact tie the one with last bit 0 is taken. The parity constraint avoids a long chain of roundings always pushing the result in the same direction, accumulating error.
- **Towards zero** (truncation), **towards +∞**, **towards −∞**: used mainly in interval arithmetic, where a guaranteed bound is needed.

To round correctly it is not enough to look at the first discarded bit: the hardware keeps three extra working bits — **guard**, **round** and **sticky** — where the third remembers “there was still something beyond”, which is what distinguishes an exact tie from a value just above.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/intervallo-e-precisione">

## Range and precision

In single precision the stored exponent goes from 1 to 254 (0 and 255 are reserved for the special cases), so e goes from −126 to +127:

```
smallest normalised number ≈ 1.18 × 10⁻³⁸
largest number             ≈ 3.40 × 10³⁸
significant decimal digits  ≈ 7

double precision:  ≈ 10⁻³⁰⁸ … 10³⁰⁸,  ≈ 16 digits
```

The point to grasp is that precision is **relative**, not absolute: between 1 and 2 the representable numbers are about 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ apart; between one million and two million they are more than 0.06 apart. The larger the number, the sparser the representable values.

Two consequences the exam asks about often. Adding a tiny number to a huge one may change nothing (**absorption**): the rounded result goes back to the starting value. And floating-point addition is **not associative**: (a + b) + c can differ from a + (b + c), so changing the order of the sums changes the result — a fact that every numerical program has to live with.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/doppia-precisione-64-bit">

## Double precision (64 bits)

1 sign bit, **11** exponent bits with bias **1023**, **52** mantissa bits. Same logic, more range and more precision.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/valori-speciali">

## Special values

Two exponent configurations are reserved:

- `E = 0` with zero mantissa → **zero** (signed: +0 and −0 both exist).
- `E = 0` with non-zero mantissa → **denormal** numbers, without the hidden bit: they fill the gap around zero.
- `E = 255` (all 1s) with zero mantissa → **infinity**, signed.
- `E = 255` with non-zero mantissa → **NaN**, the result of undefined operations such as 0/0.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/virgola-mobile/da-ricordare">

## To remember

The standard is called **IEEE 754**. Two practical consequences: floating-point addition is **not associative**, because every step rounds; and many “simple” decimals such as 0.1 have no finite binary representation, so equality comparison between floats is unreliable.

</div>

## Encoding a number — example

Suppose we want to represent `−6.5`:

1. In binary: `6.5 = 110.1₂`
2. Normalise: `110.1 = 1.101 × 2²` → mantissa `101`, real exponent `2`
3. Bias: `E = 2 + 127 = 129 = 10000001₂`
4. Sign: negative → `S = 1`

```
1 10000001 10100000000000000000000
```

To **decode** you proceed backwards: subtract the bias from the exponent, put the implicit 1 back in front of the mantissa and move the point.

## Common mistakes

- Storing the 1 before the point too: it is **implicit**, only the digits after it go into the mantissa.
- Subtracting the bias instead of adding it when **encoding**: you add when writing, subtract when reading.
- Normalising badly: the form is always `1.xxx × 2ᵉ`, with a single non-zero digit before the point.
- Treating an exponent of all 0s or all 1s as normal numbers: they are the special cases (zero, denormals, infinity, NaN).

</div>
