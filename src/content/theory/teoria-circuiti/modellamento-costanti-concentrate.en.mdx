<Layer kind="intuizione">

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-intuizione-1">

Picture looking at a circuit "from far away": if the signal changes slowly compared with the time it takes to travel across the circuit, every point of a wire sees the same current at the same instant. Each component then boils down to two numbers at its terminals, voltage and current, and geometry no longer matters: only the way the pieces are connected does.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-1">

A physical circuit is a set of devices joined by conductors. The **lumped-parameter model** replaces each device with an ideal element (resistor, capacitor, inductor, source…) and each connection with an ideal conductor, so that voltage and current are well-defined quantities at the terminals.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-2">

The key assumption is that propagation effects are negligible: if $L$ is the largest dimension of the circuit and $\lambda = c/f$ the wavelength ($c\approx 3\cdot10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$), the model is reasonable when $L \ll \lambda$, i.e. when the propagation time $L/c$ is much shorter than the period $T=1/f$ of the signal.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-3">

In this regime electric and magnetic fields are confined inside the components: electric energy is "lumped" in capacitors, magnetic energy in inductors, dissipation in resistors. Two facts follow: the current has the same value at the input and at the output of a conductor (Kirchhoff's laws hold), and the voltage between two points does not depend on the path chosen outside the components.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-4">

**Orders of magnitude.** At $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$: $\lambda = 6000\,\mathrm{km}$, so a bench circuit is lumped, while a transmission line hundreds of km long is not. At $1\,\mathrm{GHz}$: $\lambda = 30\,\mathrm{cm}$, and even a $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ cable is no longer a simple wire.

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio">

<div class="worked" data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-esempio-1">

### When is a circuit "lumped"?

A circuit whose largest dimension is $L = 20\,\mathrm{cm}$ operates at $f = 10\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Can the lumped-parameter model be used?

**Solution.**

1. $\lambda = c/f = 3\cdot10^{8}/10^{7} = 30\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. $L/\lambda = 0.2/30 \approx 0.0067 \ll 1$: the assumption is well satisfied (usually at least $L/\lambda \lesssim 0.1$ is required).
3. With the same size, at $f=1\,\mathrm{GHz}$ we would have $\lambda=0.3\,\mathrm{m}$ and $L/\lambda \approx 0.67$: the model is no longer valid.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="errori">

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-errore-1">

- **Wrong:** Applying Kirchhoff's laws to a long line or at high frequency "because they always hold".
- **Right:** Kirchhoff's laws follow from the lumped-parameter model: apply them only if $L \ll \lambda$.

</div>
