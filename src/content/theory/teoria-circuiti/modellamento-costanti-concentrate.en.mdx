<Layer kind="intuizione">

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-intuizione-1">

Picture looking at a circuit "from far away": if the signal changes slowly compared with the time it takes to travel across the circuit, every point of a wire sees the same current at the same instant. Each component then boils down to two numbers at its terminals, voltage and current, and geometry no longer matters: only the way the pieces are connected does.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-intuizione-2">

Another way to see it: a $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ signal has a period of $20\,\mathrm{ms}$, while light crosses a lab bench in a few nanoseconds. During that time the signal is practically frozen: it is like taking a snapshot of the circuit instant by instant. When the period gets close to the crossing time the "snapshot" is blurred: different values of current and voltage coexist along the same wire, and the field equations (or transmission-line equations) are needed.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-1">

A physical circuit is a set of devices joined by conductors. The **lumped-parameter model** replaces each device with an ideal element (resistor, capacitor, inductor, source…) and each connection with an ideal conductor, so that voltage and current are well-defined quantities at the terminals.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-2">

The key assumption is that propagation effects are negligible: if $L$ is the largest dimension of the circuit and $\lambda = c/f$ the wavelength ($c\approx 3\cdot10^{8}\,\mathrm{m/s}$), the model is reasonable when $L \ll \lambda$, i.e. when the propagation time $L/c$ is much shorter than the period $T=1/f$ of the signal.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-3">

In this regime electric and magnetic fields are confined inside the components: electric energy is "lumped" in capacitors, magnetic energy in inductors, dissipation in resistors. Two facts follow: the current has the same value at the input and at the output of a conductor (Kirchhoff's laws hold), and the voltage between two points does not depend on the path chosen outside the components.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-4">

**Orders of magnitude.** At $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$: $\lambda = 6000\,\mathrm{km}$, so a bench circuit is lumped, while a transmission line hundreds of km long is not. At $1\,\mathrm{GHz}$: $\lambda = 30\,\mathrm{cm}$, and even a $10\,\mathrm{cm}$ cable is no longer a simple wire.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-5">

**What is lost.** In the lumped model the quantities depend only on time, $v(t)$ and $i(t)$, not on position: the circuit equations are algebraic and ordinary differential equations. In a distributed model (transmission line) voltage and current also depend on the coordinate $x$ along the conductor, and partial differential equations appear. The border between the two is the comparison between $L$ and $\lambda$ or, equivalently, between the propagation delay $\tau_p=L/c$ and the period $T$: indeed $\tau_p/T=L/\lambda$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-teoria-6">

**Non-sinusoidal signals.** For a square wave or a pulse what matters is the highest significant frequency component, not the fundamental: the harmonics of a $100\,\mathrm{MHz}$ clock reach hundreds of megahertz. A practical estimate of the bandwidth of a rising edge of duration $t_r$ is $f\approx0.35/t_r$ <span class="badge badge--warning">TO BE VERIFIED</span> (a rule of thumb, not in the course sources).

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio">

<div class="worked" data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-esempio-1">

### When is a circuit "lumped"?

A circuit whose largest dimension is $L = 20\,\mathrm{cm}$ operates at $f = 10\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Can the lumped-parameter model be used?

**Solution.**

1. $\lambda = c/f = 3\cdot10^{8}/10^{7} = 30\,\mathrm{m}$.
2. $L/\lambda = 0.2/30 \approx 0.0067 \ll 1$: the assumption is well satisfied (usually at least $L/\lambda \lesssim 0.1$ is required).
3. With the same size, at $f=1\,\mathrm{GHz}$ we would have $\lambda=0.3\,\mathrm{m}$ and $L/\lambda \approx 0.67$: the model is no longer valid.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-esempio-2">

### A printed-circuit trace

A trace of length $L=15\,\mathrm{cm}$ carries a clock at $f=100\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Does the lumped model hold at the fundamental? And at the third harmonic?

**Solution.**

1. Fundamental: $\lambda=c/f=3\cdot10^8/10^8=3\,\mathrm m$, so $L/\lambda=0.15/3=0.05$: below the practical threshold $0.1$.
2. Third harmonic, $300\,\mathrm{MHz}$: $\lambda=1\,\mathrm m$ and $L/\lambda=0.15$: the criterion is no longer met.
3. Check with times: $\tau_p=L/c=0.15/(3\cdot10^8)=0.5\,\mathrm{ns}$ and $T=10\,\mathrm{ns}$, so $\tau_p/T=0.05=L/\lambda$ ✓. Conclusion: for the clock edges the trace must be treated as a line.

</div>

<Layer kind="errori">

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-errore-1">

- **Wrong:** Applying Kirchhoff's laws to a long line or at high frequency "because they always hold".
- **Right:** Kirchhoff's laws follow from the lumped-parameter model: apply them only if $L \ll \lambda$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-errore-2">

- **Wrong:** Judging a circuit "lumped" from the frequency alone ("$10\,\mathrm{MHz}$ is low").
- **Right:** What matters is the ratio $L/\lambda$: at $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$ a $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ circuit is lumped, a $30\,\mathrm m$ line is not.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-errore-3">

- **Wrong:** Evaluating $\lambda$ only at the fundamental of a square wave or a pulse.
- **Right:** The high harmonics (steep edges) have a shorter $\lambda$: the criterion must be checked at the highest significant frequency.

</div>

<div data-note-id="modellamento-costanti-concentrate-errore-4">

- **Wrong:** Writing $\lambda=c\,f$.
- **Right:** $\lambda=c/f$: a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength (unit check: $\mathrm{m/s}$ divided by $\mathrm{Hz}$ gives metres).

</div>
