<Layer kind="intuizione">

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-intuizione-1">

An instantaneous "kick" at the input makes the circuit ring according to its nature: that response is its fingerprint. Any input can be seen as a train of successive kicks, and convolution adds up their responses.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-intuizione-2">

The step response is easier to picture: it is what happens when a constant source is suddenly switched on. The impulse is the derivative of the step, so the impulse response is the derivative of the step response: equivalent information, presented in the form that suits convolution.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-teoria-1">

The **impulse response** $h(t)$ is the output for $x=\delta(t)$ with zero initial conditions. For a causal system $h(t)=0$ for $t<0$. The output for any input (zero initial conditions) is the **convolution** $y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau$. The step response is the integral of $h$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-teoria-2">

The link with the network function is $h(t)=\mathcal L^{-1}\{H(s)\}$: the poles of $H$ determine the modes of $h$ and hence stability (if $h$ is absolutely integrable the system is stable).

</div>

<details class="proof" data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-dimostrazione-1">
<summary>Proof: Impulse response and network function</summary>

**Statement.** $h(t)=\mathcal L^{-1}\{H(s)\}$ and $y=h*x$.

1. For $x=\delta(t)$ we have $X(s)=1$.
2. With zero initial conditions $Y(s)=H(s)X(s)=H(s)$.
3. Inverting, $y=h=\mathcal L^{-1}\{H\}$.
4. For any input $Y=HX$; since $\mathcal L\{h*x\}=HX$ (convolution property), $y=h*x$. $\square$

</details>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-teoria-3">

**Why convolution.** Any input can be approximated by a train of impulses of area $x(\tau)\,\Delta\tau$ at the instants $\tau$. By linearity and time invariance each produces $x(\tau)\,\Delta\tau\,h(t-\tau)$; summing and taking the limit gives $y(t)=\int_0^tx(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau$. The factor $h(t-\tau)$ weighs the past: in a stable system distant inputs count less and less.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-teoria-4">

**Convolution and Laplace.** In the $s$ domain convolution becomes a product, $Y(s)=H(s)X(s)$: it is usually easier to multiply and invert than to compute the integral. The integral remains useful for piecewise inputs and for reasoning about causality.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-teoria-5">

**Useful properties.** Convolution is commutative, $x*h=h*x$; the step response is the integral of the impulse response, $y_{st}(t)=\int_0^th(\tau)\,d\tau$; for a stable system its final value is $H(0)=\int_0^\infty h(t)\,dt$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-teoria-6">

**Causality and stability read on $h$.** A physical circuit is causal, so $h(t)=0$ for $t\lt 0$: the output cannot anticipate the impulse. It is stable if $h$ dies out fast enough to have a finite absolute area; an $h$ that oscillates without damping, or grows, indicates a marginal or unstable circuit.

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio">

<div class="worked" data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-esempio-1">

### Impulse response of an RC

$H=\dfrac{1}{s+1}$.

**Solution.**

1. $h(t)=e^{-t}u_{-1}(t)$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-esempio-2">

### Step response

Same system, input $u_{-1}$.

**Solution.**

1. $y=\int_0^th=1-e^{-t}$ for $t>0$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-esempio-3">

### Rectangular pulse

Same system, input $1$ for $0<t<1$.

**Solution.**

1. $0<t<1$: $y=1-e^{-t}$.
2. $t>1$: $y=(1-e^{-1})e^{-(t-1)}$ (continuity at $t=1$).

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-esempio-4">

### Convolution of two exponentials

$h(t)=e^{-2t}u_{-1}(t)$ and input $x(t)=e^{-t}u_{-1}(t)$. Compute $y=x*h$ with the integral and check with Laplace.

**Solution.**

1. $y(t)=\int_0^te^{-\tau}e^{-2(t-\tau)}d\tau=e^{-2t}\int_0^te^{\tau}d\tau=e^{-2t}(e^t-1)=e^{-t}-e^{-2t}$.
2. Laplace: $Y=\dfrac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)}=\dfrac1{s+1}-\dfrac1{s+2}$ ✓.
3. Maximum: $\dot y=-e^{-t}+2e^{-2t}=0\Rightarrow t=\ln2\approx0.69\,\mathrm s$, $y_{max}=0.5-0.25=0.25$.

</div>

<Layer kind="errori">

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-errore-1">

- **Wrong:** Deriving $h$ from the step response without differentiating.
- **Right:** $h=\dfrac{d}{dt}y_{step}$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-errore-2">

- **Wrong:** Writing $y=h\cdot x$ in time, as a product.
- **Right:** In time it is a convolution; the product holds between transforms, $Y=HX$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-errore-3">

- **Wrong:** Using convolution with nonzero initial conditions and forgetting the free response.
- **Right:** $y=h*x$ is only the forced response: with nonzero initial conditions the free response must be added.

</div>

<div data-note-id="risposta-impulsiva-convoluzione-errore-4">

- **Wrong:** Forgetting that in convolution the argument of $h$ is $t-\tau$.
- **Right:** One integrates over $\tau$ the product $x(\tau)\,h(t-\tau)$: writing $h(\tau)$ loses the dependence on $t$ and the result is a constant.

</div>
