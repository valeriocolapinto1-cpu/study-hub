<Layer kind="intuizione">

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-intuizione-1">

A resistor reacts to the present: the current now depends on the voltage now. Capacitors and inductors instead remember: they store charge or flux, and changing their state takes time. This is why the voltage across a capacitor and the current in an inductor cannot jump: they are the "memory" of the circuit.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-intuizione-2">

Continuity holds as long as the quantities stay bounded: a jump in $v_C$ would require an impulsive current in the capacitor, a jump in $i_L$ an impulsive voltage across the inductor. In circuits with ordinary resistors and sources this does not happen; the exceptions are connections between inconsistent ideal elements, such as two capacitors at different voltages put in parallel (see the note in the formalism).

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-1">

**Resistor.** $v = R\,i$, i.e. $i = G\,v$ with $G=1/R$. Short circuit: $R=0$ ($v=0$ for every $i$). Open circuit: $G=0$ ($i=0$ for every $v$).

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-2">

**Capacitor.** $i = C\,\dfrac{dv}{dt}$, and $v(t) = v(t_0) + \dfrac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t} i\,d\tau$. In DC ($dv/dt=0$) it behaves as an open circuit.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-3">

**Inductor.** $v = L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$, and $i(t) = i(t_0)+\dfrac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t} v\,d\tau$. In DC it is a short circuit.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-4">

**Ideal sources.** The voltage source imposes $v=e(t)$ for every current; the current source imposes $i=j(t)$ for every voltage. With zero value, the former is a short circuit, the latter an open circuit.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-5">

These elements are _circuits with memory_ ($C$, $L$: the response depends on the past) or _memoryless_ ($R$, sources).

</div>

<details class="proof" data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-dimostrazione-1">
<summary>Proof: Continuity of the inductor current and of the capacitor voltage</summary>

**Statement.** If the voltage $v$ across an inductor is bounded in a neighbourhood of $t_0$, the current $i_L$ is continuous at $t_0$. Likewise $v_C$ is continuous if $i_C$ is bounded.

1. From the constitutive relation: $i_L(t_0+\varepsilon)-i_L(t_0-\varepsilon) = \dfrac{1}{L}\int_{t_0-\varepsilon}^{t_0+\varepsilon} v\,d\tau$.
2. If $|v|\le M$ in the neighbourhood, the magnitude of the right-hand side is $\le 2M\varepsilon/L$.
3. As $\varepsilon\to0$ the right-hand side tends to zero, hence $i_L(t_0^+)=i_L(t_0^-)$. The proof for $v_C$ is identical with the roles exchanged ($v_C=\frac1C\int i\,d\tau$). $\square$

_Note._ If $v$ contains impulses (cases with inconsistent ideal elements) continuity may fail: see <span class="badge badge--warning">TO BE VERIFIED</span> the lecturer's treatment.

</details>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-6">

**Duality.** The relations of capacitor and inductor swap under $v\leftrightarrow i$ and $C\leftrightarrow L$: $i=C\,dv/dt$ becomes $v=L\,di/dt$. The same correspondence links series and parallel, short and open circuit, voltage source and current source. With duality one result follows from the other without redoing the algebra.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-7">

**Sign convention.** The relations $v=Ri$, $i=C\,dv/dt$, $v=L\,di/dt$ are written with the passive sign convention. With the active convention a minus sign appears: $v=-Ri$, $i=-C\,dv/dt$, $v=-L\,di/dt$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-teoria-8">

**Memory and state.** The voltage of a capacitor at $t$ depends on all the past current, $v(t)=\frac1C\int_{-\infty}^{t} i\,d\tau$; but the value $v(t_0)$ summarises the whole past up to $t_0$. This is why $v_C$ and $i_L$ are called _state variables_, and in a circuit without inconsistencies their number is the order of the circuit.

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio">

<div class="worked" data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-esempio-1">

### Charging a capacitor with a constant current

A capacitor $C = 2\,\mathrm{F}$, initially uncharged, carries $i = 3\,\mathrm{A}$ for $4\,\mathrm{s}$. Find the final $v$ and the stored energy.

**Solution.**

1. $v(4)=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_0^4 3\,d\tau = \dfrac{12}{2}=6\,\mathrm{V}$.
2. $W = \tfrac12 C v^2 = \tfrac12\cdot 2\cdot 36 = 36\,\mathrm{J}$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-esempio-2">

### Inductor with a ramp current

An inductor $L=0.5\,\mathrm H$ carries $i(t)=4t\,\mathrm A$ for $0\lt t\lt 2\,\mathrm s$, then the current stays constant. Find the voltage and the energy stored at $t=2\,\mathrm s$.

**Solution.**

1. During the ramp $v=L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}=0.5\cdot4=2\,\mathrm V$; after $t=2\,\mathrm s$ the current is constant and $v=0$.
2. At $t=2\,\mathrm s$: $i=8\,\mathrm A$, $W_L=\tfrac12 L i^2=\tfrac12\cdot0.5\cdot64=16\,\mathrm J$.
3. Check: $\int_0^2 v\,i\,dt=\int_0^2 2\cdot4t\,dt=4t^2\big|_0^2=16\,\mathrm J$ ✓. The voltage jumps from $2$ to $0\,\mathrm V$ at $t=2\,\mathrm s$, the current does not.

</div>

<Layer kind="errori">

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-errore-1">

- **Wrong:** Imposing $v_C(0^+)=0$ for $t>0$ "because the source is switched off".
- **Right:** $v_C$ is continuous: $v_C(0^+)=v_C(0^-)$ is the value reached in the previous steady state.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-errore-2">

- **Wrong:** Treating a capacitor in DC as a short circuit.
- **Right:** In DC $dv/dt=0$ so $i=0$: the capacitor is an open circuit; it is the inductor that behaves as a short.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-errore-3">

- **Wrong:** Imposing continuity on $i_C$ or $v_L$ as well.
- **Right:** Only $v_C$ and $i_L$, the state variables, are continuous; $i_C$ and $v_L$ can jump, as in the ramp example.

</div>

<div data-note-id="relazioni-costitutive-errore-4">

- **Wrong:** Using $v(t)=\frac1C\int_{t_0}^{t} i\,d\tau$ without the initial value.
- **Right:** $v(t)=v(t_0)+\frac1C\int_{t_0}^{t} i\,d\tau$: without $v(t_0)$ the result holds only for a capacitor discharged at $t_0$.

</div>
