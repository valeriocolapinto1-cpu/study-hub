<Layer kind="intuizione">

<div data-note-id="fasori-intuizione-1">

Every quantity of a linear circuit driven by a sinusoid oscillates at the same frequency: only amplitude and phase change. The phasor stores exactly these two pieces of information in a complex number, and turns derivatives and integrals into simple multiplications: differential equations become algebraic.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-intuizione-2">

You can picture the phasor as an arrow rotating counterclockwise in the complex plane with angular velocity $\omega$: the physical quantity is its projection on the real axis. All the arrows of a circuit rotate together, so a snapshot at $t=0$ is enough: their lengths and the angles between them are all the useful information.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-1">

To $v(t)=V_m\cos(\omega t+\varphi)$ we associate the **phasor** $\dot V=V_me^{j\varphi}$, with $v(t)=\mathrm{Re}\{\dot Ve^{j\omega t}\}$. The phasor is a constant complex number: the time dependence lies in the factor $e^{j\omega t}$, common to all quantities and left implicit.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-2">

The correspondence is linear: a sum of sinusoids at the same frequency ↔ the sum of the phasors. Derivative ↔ multiplication by $j\omega$, integral ↔ division by $j\omega$. The sine is reduced to a cosine: $\sin\omega t=\cos(\omega t-90^\circ)$, phasor $-j$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-3">

Cartesian ↔ amplitude-phase form: $a\cos\omega t+b\sin\omega t=\mathrm{Re}\{(a-jb)e^{j\omega t}\}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\cos(\omega t-\arctan(b/a))$ (for $a>0$; in general $\varphi=\mathrm{atan2}(-b,a)$).

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-4">

**Why it works.** The circuit operations (sums, derivatives, integrals, products by real constants) commute with $\mathrm{Re}\{\cdot\}$: for instance $\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Re}\{\dot Ve^{j\omega t}\}=\mathrm{Re}\{j\omega\dot Ve^{j\omega t}\}$. The product of two quantities does not: that is why the instantaneous power $p=v\,i$ is not $\mathrm{Re}\{\dot V\dot I\}$ and complex power is needed.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-5">

**Peak or RMS.** The phasor can be defined with the peak amplitude $V_m$ or with the RMS value $V=V_m/\sqrt2$. The linear relations (Kirchhoff, impedances) are identical in both cases; the power formulas change, and with RMS values they lose the factor $\tfrac12$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-6">

**Operations in polar form.** Multiplying by $\rho e^{j\theta}$ means scaling the amplitude by $\rho$ and advancing the phase by $\theta$: $j$ advances by $90^\circ$, $-j$ delays by $90^\circ$, $j\omega$ (the derivative) amplifies by $\omega$ and advances by $90^\circ$. Sums and differences, instead, are done in Cartesian form.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-teoria-7">

**RMS value.** For a sinusoid $V=V_m/\sqrt2\approx0.707\,V_m$: the "$230\,\mathrm V$" mains voltage is an RMS value, with a peak of $230\sqrt2\approx325\,\mathrm V$.

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio">

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-esempio-1">

### Sum of a sine and a cosine

Write $3\cos t+4\sin t$ in amplitude-phase form.

**Solution.**

1. Phasor: $3-4j$, magnitude $5$, phase $-\arctan(4/3)\approx-53.13^\circ$.
2. $3\cos t+4\sin t=5\cos(t-53.13^\circ)$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-esempio-2">

### Derivative of a sinusoid

$v(t)=10\cos(2t+30^\circ)$. Find $\dot v$.

**Solution.**

1. $\dot V=10e^{j30^\circ}$; $j\omega\dot V=2e^{j90^\circ}\cdot10e^{j30^\circ}=20e^{j120^\circ}$.
2. $\dot v=20\cos(2t+120^\circ)=-20\sin(2t+30^\circ)$ ✓.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-esempio-3">

### Phasor of a sine

What is the phasor of $5\sin(3t)$ in the cosine convention?

**Solution.**

1. $5\sin3t=5\cos(3t-90^\circ)$, hence $\dot V=5e^{-j90^\circ}=-5j$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-esempio-4">

### Sum of two phase-shifted sinusoids

Find amplitude and phase of $v=4\cos(\omega t+30^\circ)+3\cos(\omega t-60^\circ)$.

**Solution.**

1. Phasors in Cartesian form: $4e^{j30^\circ}=3.464+2j$, $3e^{-j60^\circ}=1.5-2.598j$.
2. Sum: $\dot V=4.964-0.598j$, $|\dot V|=\sqrt{4.964^2+0.598^2}=5.00$, phase $\arctan(-0.598/4.964)=-6.87^\circ$.
3. $v=5\cos(\omega t-6.87^\circ)$. Check: the two phasors are $90^\circ$ apart, so $|\dot V|=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5$ ✓.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="errori">

<div data-note-id="fasori-errore-1">

- **Wrong:** Forgetting that the sine has phase $-90^\circ$ with respect to the cosine and using the phasor $+5j$.
- **Right:** $\sin\omega t\leftrightarrow-j$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-errore-2">

- **Wrong:** Confusing peak and RMS amplitude: $V_m=\sqrt2V$.
- **Right:** Always state whether phasors are peak or RMS values (it changes $P$, $Q$).

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-errore-3">

- **Wrong:** Adding the amplitudes of two phase-shifted sinusoids: $4+3=7$.
- **Right:** Add the phasors: with a $90^\circ$ shift the amplitude is $\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5$. Adding amplitudes works only for sinusoids in phase.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-errore-4">

- **Wrong:** Computing the phase as $\arctan(b/a)$ even when the real part is negative.
- **Right:** With $a\lt 0$ the arctangent is off by $180^\circ$: use $\mathrm{atan2}$ or look at the quadrant (for $-1+j$ the phase is $135^\circ$, not $-45^\circ$).

</div>
