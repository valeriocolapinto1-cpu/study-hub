<Layer kind="intuizione">

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-intuizione-1">

Phasors and Laplace are not two separate worlds: phasors are Laplace "restricted" to the imaginary axis, $s=j\omega$, without initial conditions. That is why they only see the steady state, and only if a steady state exists.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-intuizione-2">

Setting $s=j\omega$ means asking how the circuit responds to a complex exponential $e^{j\omega t}$ that has "always" been there: there is no switch-on instant and therefore no transient. Laplace instead considers inputs switched on at $t=0$ and sees everything that follows, transient and steady state.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-intuizione-3">

In practice: for the sinusoidal steady state of a stable circuit computing $H(j\omega)$ with phasors is enough; to know how it is reached, from switch-on onwards, Laplace is needed.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-teoria-1">

The network function for phasors is $H(j\omega)=H(s)\big|_{s=j\omega}$, valid for stable systems. The magnitude $|H(j\omega)|$ and the phase $\arg H(j\omega)$ tell how the steady-state sinusoidal output is scaled and shifted with respect to the input.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-teoria-2">

The phasor equations are the Laplace ones with $s=j\omega$ and _without_ initial conditions: the phasor method only sees the steady state.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-teoria-3">

**Why it holds.** For a stable system with input $\cos\omega t$ switched on at $t=0$, the expansion of $Y(s)=H(s)X(s)$ contains the terms of the poles of $H$, which die out, and those of the input poles $\pm j\omega$, whose coefficient is proportional to $H(j\omega)$. As $t\to\infty$ what remains is $|H(j\omega)|\cos(\omega t+\arg H(j\omega))$: exactly the phasor result.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-teoria-4">

**Element by element.** The correspondence also holds for impedances: $sL\to j\omega L$, $\dfrac{1}{sC}\to\dfrac{1}{j\omega C}$. Every phasor method (nodes, meshes, Thévenin) is the corresponding $s$-domain method evaluated at $s=j\omega$ and without the initial-condition sources.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-teoria-5">

**What is lost.** Phasors do not show the transient, the initial conditions or non-sinusoidal inputs (steps, impulses). Laplace shows everything, but for the steady state the input poles must be expanded too: if only the sinusoidal steady state matters, phasors are quicker.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-teoria-6">

**Magnitude and phase from poles and zeros.** Writing $H(s)=K\dfrac{\prod(s-z_i)}{\prod(s-p_k)}$, at $s=j\omega$ the magnitude is $|K|$ times the product of the distances from $j\omega$ to the zeros divided by the product of the distances to the poles; the phase is the sum of the angles to the zeros minus the sum of the angles to the poles. This is why, approaching a lightly damped pole, the magnitude grows.

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio">

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-esempio-1">

### Magnitude and phase

$H=\dfrac{1}{s+1}$ at $\omega=1$.

**Solution.**

1. $H(j)=\dfrac1{1+j}$: $|H|=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\approx0.707$, $\arg=-45^\circ$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-esempio-2">

### High-pass

$H=\dfrac{s}{s+1}$ at $\omega=1$.

**Solution.**

1. $\dfrac{j}{1+j}=\dfrac{j(1-j)}2=\dfrac{1+j}{2}$: $|H|=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, $\arg=+45^\circ$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-esempio-3">

### Steady-state output

$x=4\cos t$ in the first system.

**Solution.**

1. $y_p=4\cdot0.707\cos(t-45^\circ)\approx2.83\cos(t-45^\circ)$.

</div>

<div class="worked" data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-esempio-4">

### Steady state of a second-order system

$H(s)=\dfrac{10}{s^2+2s+10}$ with input $x=2\cos3t$. Find the steady-state output.

**Solution.**

1. Stability: poles $-1\pm3j$, negative real part: the steady state exists.
2. $H(3j)=\dfrac{10}{10-9+6j}=\dfrac{10}{1+6j}$: $|H|=\dfrac{10}{\sqrt{37}}=1.644$, $\arg H=-\arctan6=-80.5^\circ$.
3. $y_p=2\cdot1.644\cos(3t-80.5^\circ)=3.29\cos(3t-80.5^\circ)$: near the frequency of the poles the amplitude is amplified.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="errori">

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-errore-1">

- **Wrong:** Using $H(j\omega)$ for unstable systems.
- **Right:** The relation holds only with poles with negative real part.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-errore-2">

- **Wrong:** Substituting $s=\omega$ instead of $s=j\omega$.
- **Right:** The sinusoidal response is read on the imaginary axis, $s=j\omega$: with $s=\omega$ one evaluates $H$ on the real axis, which is irrelevant.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-errore-3">

- **Wrong:** Using $H(j\omega)$ for the response right after switch-on.
- **Right:** $H(j\omega)$ gives only the steady state; for the first instants the whole $Y(s)$ is needed, transient included.

</div>

<div data-note-id="fasori-e-laplace-errore-4">

- **Wrong:** Using phasors when the input frequency coincides with a pole on the imaginary axis.
- **Right:** Then $H(j\omega)$ is infinite and the steady state does not exist: the response grows in time, as in the ideal LC circuit at its resonance.

</div>
