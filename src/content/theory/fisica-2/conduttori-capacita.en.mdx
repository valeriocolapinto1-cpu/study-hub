<Layer kind="intuizione">

In a conductor charges are free to move: they rearrange until they have cancelled the field inside the material. Everything said about conductors in equilibrium follows from this fact: charge only on the surface, equipotential volume, external field perpendicular to the surface.

A **capacitor** stores charge and energy by placing two conductors with opposite charges close to each other. The capacitance $C=Q/\Delta V$ depends only on the geometry and is always found with the same scheme (field from Gauss, integral for $\Delta V$, ratio). The energy spent charging it stays "in the field", with density $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$.

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo">

## Conductors in equilibrium

In a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium the free charges are at rest, therefore:

- the field inside the material is **zero**;
- the excess charge lies only on the **surface** (by Gauss $\rho=0$ inside);
- the conductor is an **equipotential** volume and the external field is perpendicular to the surface.

<Diagram
  type="charge-distribution"
  params={{ shape: 'sphere', conductor: true, gaussian: 0.6 }}
  caption="Charged conductor in equilibrium: the charge sits on the surface and an inner Gaussian surface encloses no charge, so E = 0."
/>

**Coulomb's theorem.** With a pillbox straddling the surface (flux only through the outer face): $E\,dS = \sigma\,dS/\varepsilon_0$, that is

$$
\vec E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\,\hat n
$$

**Point effect.** Two spheres of radii $R_1$, $R_2$ joined by a wire are at the same potential: $\frac{Q_1}{R_1}=\frac{Q_2}{R_2}$, hence $\sigma_1R_1=\sigma_2R_2$. The charge density, and so the field, is larger where the radius of curvature is small (tips): there the air can become conducting (lightning rods, corona discharge).

**Electrostatic shielding.** In an empty cavity of a conductor the field is zero, whatever the external field (Faraday cage). If the cavity contains a charge $q$, a charge $-q$ is induced on the inner wall and $+q$ appears on the outer surface (complete induction); grounding the conductor cancels the outer charge.

## Capacitance and capacitors

For an isolated conductor $Q$ and $V$ are proportional: $C = Q/V$ (farad). Sphere: $C=4\pi\varepsilon_0R$; the Earth ($R\simeq6.4\times10^6$ m) has $C\simeq0.7$ mF. For a system of conductors one defines the _coefficients of potential_ $V_i=\sum_j p_{ij}Q_j$ and of _induction_ $Q_i=\sum_j c_{ij}V_j$ (symmetric matrices).

<Diagram
  type="capacitor"
  params={{ geometry: 'parallel', fringe: true }}
  caption="Parallel-plate capacitor: uniform field between the plates; the lines bend at the edges."
/>

A **capacitor** consists of two conductors in complete induction with charges $\pm Q$: $C = Q/\Delta V$. Procedure: compute $E$ between the plates with Gauss, integrate to get $\Delta V$, divide.

- Parallel-plate: $E=\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, $\Delta V = Qd/(\varepsilon_0S)$ ⇒ $C=\dfrac{\varepsilon_0S}{d}$.
- Spherical: $\Delta V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}\right)$ ⇒ $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\dfrac{R_1R_2}{R_2-R_1}$.
- Cylindrical (length $L$): $\Delta V = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1}$ ⇒ $C = \dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon_0L}{\ln(R_2/R_1)}$.

**Connections.** In parallel they have the same $\Delta V$ and the charges add: $C_p=\sum C_i$. In series they carry the same charge and the voltages add: $1/C_s=\sum 1/C_i$.

## Electrostatic energy

The work needed to assemble a set of point charges is $U=\frac12\sum_i q_iV_i = \sum_{i\lt j}\frac{q_iq_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{ij}}$. For a system of conductors $U=\frac12\sum Q_iV_i$; for a capacitor, charging it with $dW = V\,dq = \frac{q}{C}dq$:

$$
U = \frac{Q^2}{2C}=\frac12 CV^2 = \frac12QV
$$

In the parallel-plate capacitor $U=\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2\,(Sd)$: the energy is distributed in the field with density

$$
u = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2,\qquad U=\int u\,dV
$$

(for example, the energy of a charged sphere is obtained by integrating $u$ over all space).

## Mechanical actions on conductors

An outward **electrostatic pressure** $p = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0}=\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ acts on the surface of a conductor (the field acting on the surface charge is the average of the values inside and outside, $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$). The plates of a parallel-plate capacitor attract each other with $F=\frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0S}$.

**Method of virtual work.** At constant charge (isolated system) $F_x=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)_Q$; at constant potential (connected to a generator) $F_x=+\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)_V$, because the generator supplies twice the change in the capacitor's energy.

## The general problem of electrostatics

Given the charges and the potentials (or the charges) of the conductors, the potential is the solution of Poisson's equation with those boundary conditions, and the solution is **unique**. That is why it is enough to "guess" a solution that satisfies the conditions. Example: the _method of image charges_. A charge $q$ at distance $d$ from a grounded conducting plane produces in the half-space the same field as $q$ together with a symmetric charge $-q$; the charge is attracted with $F=\frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(2d)^2}$.

<Callout tone="esame" title="At the exam">
  In problems with conducting slabs inserted between the plates you get capacitors in series, and the
  thickness of the slab is subtracted from $d$. If a capacitor stays connected to the generator, $V$ is
  constant; if it is isolated, $Q$ is constant. Always ask yourself which of the two stays fixed.
</Callout>

## Worked example: conducting slab in a parallel-plate capacitor

A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area $S=100\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ at distance $d=2\ \mathrm{mm}$ and is charged to $V_0=100\ \mathrm V$. A conducting slab of thickness $s=1\ \mathrm{mm}$ is inserted parallel to the plates. We compare the isolated capacitor with the capacitor connected to the generator.

1. **Capacitance.** Before, $C_0=\varepsilon_0S/d\simeq 44.3\ \mathrm{pF}$. Inside the slab the field is zero: two capacitors in series remain, with total gap $d-s$, so $C=\varepsilon_0S/(d-s)\simeq 88.5\ \mathrm{pF}$, independent of the position of the slab.
2. **Isolated capacitor ($Q$ constant).** $Q=C_0V_0\simeq 4.43\ \mathrm{nC}$; after insertion $V=Q/C=50\ \mathrm V$. The energy goes from $U_0=\frac12C_0V_0^2\simeq 221\ \mathrm{nJ}$ to $U=Q^2/2C\simeq 111\ \mathrm{nJ}$: it decreases, so the slab is **pulled** in.
3. **Connected capacitor ($V$ constant).** The charge rises to $Q'=CV_0\simeq 8.85\ \mathrm{nC}$ and the energy to $U'=\frac12CV_0^2\simeq 443\ \mathrm{nJ}$. The generator supplies $\Delta Q\,V_0\simeq 443\ \mathrm{nJ}$, twice $\Delta U\simeq 221\ \mathrm{nJ}$: half goes into the capacitor's energy, the other half is the work done on the slab, which is pulled in in this case too.

**Moral.** Before using $U=Q^2/2C$ or $U=\frac12CV^2$, decide which quantity stays fixed.

<Layer kind="errori">

## Common mistakes

- **Putting charge inside the volume of a conductor.** At equilibrium the excess charge lies only on the surface; inside the material $\vec E=0$.
- **Using $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ next to a conductor.** Just outside the surface of a conductor the field is $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (Coulomb's theorem); $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ is the field of an isolated plane, or the field acting on the surface charge.
- **Mixing up series and parallel.** Same charge → series (the $1/C$ add); same voltage → parallel (the $C$ add).
- **Virtual work with the wrong sign.** At constant $Q$, $F_x=-\partial U/\partial x$; at constant $V$, $F_x=+\partial U/\partial x$: the generator supplies twice the change in energy.
- **Forgetting complete induction.** With a charge $q$ in the cavity there is $-q$ on the inner wall and an extra $+q$ on the outer surface, on top of the shell's initial charge.
