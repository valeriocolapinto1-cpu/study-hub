<Layer kind="intuizione" title="The idea in brief">

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/idea">

Work measures how much 'useful push' a force gives along the motion: only the component of the force along the velocity changes the speed. The kinetic energy theorem is Newton's second law 'integrated along the path': it turns instantaneous information (F = ma) into a balance between two states, often much handier than calculations with accelerations.

</div>

</Layer>

<Layer kind="formalismo" title="Definitions and derivation">

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/definizioni">

## Definitions

The elementary work of a force $\vec{F}$ over an infinitesimal displacement $d\vec{r}$ is $dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = F\,ds\cos\theta$; the work along a path is the line integral $W = \int_A^B \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}$. The kinetic energy of a point particle is $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. The kinetic energy theorem (or work–energy theorem) states that the total work of all the acting forces equals the change in kinetic energy: $W_{tot} = \Delta K$. Power is $P = dW/dt = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}$.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/derivazione" class="derivation">

## Step-by-step derivation

<p class="muted">Try deriving it aloud first, then open one step at a time.</p>

<details id="passo-1">
<summary>Step 1 · Start from Newton's second law</summary>

$$
\vec{F}_{tot} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}
$$

F_tot is the resultant of all the forces on the point particle.

</details>

<details id="passo-2">
<summary>Step 2 · Dot-multiply by the elementary displacement</summary>

$$
\vec{F}_{tot}\cdot d\vec{r} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}\cdot \vec{v}\,dt
$$

Using dr = v dt, the elementary work becomes a purely kinematic quantity.

</details>

<details id="passo-3">
<summary>Step 3 · Recognise an exact derivative</summary>

$$
m\,\vec{v}\cdot\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\Big(\frac{1}{2}m\,\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}\Big) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\Big)
$$

Rule for differentiating a dot product: v·dv/dt is half the derivative of v².

</details>

<details id="passo-4">
<summary>Step 4 · Integrate along the trajectory from A to B</summary>

$$
W_{tot} = \int_A^B \vec{F}_{tot}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{t_A}^{t_B} \frac{d}{dt}\Big(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\Big)dt = \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_A^2
$$

The result depends only on the initial and final speeds: W_tot = ΔK, whatever the forces and the path.

</details>

<details id="passo-5">
<summary>Step 5 · Remarks on the sign and on normal forces</summary>

$$
dW > 0 \iff \theta < 90°; \qquad \vec{F}\perp\vec{v} \Rightarrow W = 0
$$

A force always perpendicular to the velocity (tension in the pendulum, normal force, centripetal force, Lorentz force) does no work and does not change the speed.

</details>

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/ipotesi">

## Validity assumptions

Point particle (for extended bodies the theorem must be applied with care: what matters is the displacement of the point of application); classical mechanics; the theorem holds for ANY kind of force, conservative or not.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/casi-limite">

## Limiting cases

Constant force parallel to a straight displacement: W = Fs (elementary formula). Perpendicular force: W = 0, uniform circular motion. If W_tot &lt; 0 the body slows down (e.g. braking: W_friction = −ΔK).

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio" title="Worked example">

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/esempio">

A car of mass m = 1200 kg travels at 90 km/h and brakes to a stop on a straight horizontal stretch. Find: (a) the total work of the forces; (b) the braking distance if the braking force is constant and equal to 6000 N; (c) the average power dissipated if braking lasts 5.0 s.

1. **Kinetic energy theorem.** 90 km/h = 25 m/s. $W_{tot} = \Delta K = 0 - \tfrac12 \cdot 1200 \cdot 25^2 = -3.75\cdot 10^5\ \text{J}$.
2. **Braking distance.** Weight and normal force do no work; only the braking force remains: $-F d = \Delta K \Rightarrow d = 3.75\cdot 10^5/6000 = 62.5\ \text{m}$.
3. **Average power.** $\bar P = |W|/\Delta t = 3.75\cdot 10^5/5.0 = 7.5\cdot 10^4\ \text{W}$ (75 kW, turned into heat in the brakes).

</div>

<Layer kind="errori" title="Common mistakes">

<div data-note-id="teoria/lavoro-energia-cinetica/errori">

- Applying ΔK = W of only the force of interest and forgetting the others: the theorem is about the TOTAL work.
- Saying that a 'large' force always does work: if it is perpendicular to the displacement the work is zero.
- Treating work as a quantity that depends only on the initial and final positions: in general it depends on the path (except for conservative forces).
- Forgetting that work is a signed scalar, not a vector.

</div>
