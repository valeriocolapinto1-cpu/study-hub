<Layer kind="intuizione" title="The idea in brief">

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/idea">

Resonance is pushing a swing at the right moment: if every push arrives in phase with the velocity, all the power goes into the system and the amplitude grows until friction losses balance the input. That is why the phase at resonance is π/2: the force is synchronised with the velocity, not with the position.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/definizioni">

## Definitions

A driven oscillator is a damped oscillator also acted upon by a periodic external force $F(t) = F_0\cos(\Omega t)$. After a transient, the system oscillates at the angular frequency Ω of the driving force (steady-state solution) with an amplitude that depends on Ω: when Ω approaches the natural angular frequency ω₀ the amplitude grows strongly — the phenomenon of resonance. The weaker the damping, the higher and narrower the resonance peak.

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/derivazione" class="derivation">

## Step-by-step derivation

<p class="muted">Try deriving it aloud first, then open one step at a time.</p>

<details id="passo-1">
<summary>Step 1 · Equation of driven motion</summary>

$$
\ddot{x} + 2\gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\Omega t)
$$

Linear inhomogeneous equation: solution = homogeneous (transient, dies out as e^\{−γt\}) + particular (steady state).

</details>

<details id="passo-2">
<summary>Step 2 · Steady-state solution (ansatz oscillating at angular frequency Ω)</summary>

$$
x_p(t) = A(\Omega)\cos(\Omega t - \delta)
$$

In steady state the system responds at the driving frequency, not at its own; δ is the phase lag of the response behind the driving force.

</details>

<details id="passo-3">
<summary>Step 3 · Substitution and matching of coefficients</summary>

$$
A(\Omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\Omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{2\gamma\Omega}{\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2}
$$

Substitute x_p into the equation and equate the coefficients of cos and sin (or use phasors). This is the resonance curve.

</details>

<details id="passo-4">
<summary>Step 4 · Maximum of the amplitude</summary>

$$
\frac{dA}{d\Omega} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Omega_{ris} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2}, \qquad A_{max} = \frac{F_0/m}{2\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}}
$$

For weak damping Ω_res ≈ ω₀ and A_max ≈ F₀/(2mγω₀): the amplitude at resonance is limited only by damping; for γ → 0 it would diverge.

</details>

<details id="passo-5">
<summary>Step 5 · Behaviour of the phase</summary>

$$
\Omega \ll \omega_0: \delta \to 0; \qquad \Omega = \omega_0: \delta = \frac{\pi}{2}; \qquad \Omega \gg \omega_0: \delta \to \pi
$$

At low frequency the mass follows the driving force in phase; at resonance it is in quadrature (the force is in phase with the velocity: maximum power transfer); at high frequency it responds in antiphase, dominated by inertia.

</details>

<details id="passo-6">
<summary>Step 6 · Power absorbed in steady state</summary>

$$
\langle P \rangle = \frac{1}{2}F_0\,\Omega A\sin\delta \;\; \text{maximum for } \Omega = \omega_0
$$

In steady state the average power delivered by the driving force equals the power dissipated by friction: power absorption is maximum exactly at ω₀.

</details>

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/ipotesi">

## Validity assumptions

Linear system (Hooke + linear viscous friction); pure harmonic driving force; the system is observed after the transient has died out (t ≫ 1/γ).

</div>

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/casi-limite">

## Limiting cases

γ → 0: infinitely high and narrow resonance peak (in a real system the amplitude grows until non-linearities or breakage intervene). Ω → 0: A → F₀/(mω₀²) = F₀/k, the static response of the spring. Ω → ∞: A → 0, inertia prevents the system from following the force.

</div>

<Layer kind="esempio" title="Worked example">

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/esempio">

An oscillator with m = 0.20 kg, k = 80 N/m and b = 0.40 kg/s is driven by F(t) = F₀ cos(Ωt) with F₀ = 0.50 N. Find: (a) ω₀ and γ; (b) the steady-state amplitude for Ω = 15 rad/s; (c) the maximum amplitude and the angular frequency at which it occurs; (d) the phase lag at Ω = 15 rad/s.

1. **Parameters.** $\omega_0 = \sqrt{80/0.2} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$, $\gamma = b/2m = 1.0\ \text{s}^{-1}$, $F_0/m = 2.5\ \text{m/s}^2$.
2. **Amplitude at Ω = 15 rad/s.** $A = \dfrac{2.5}{\sqrt{(400-225)^2 + 4\cdot 225}} = \dfrac{2.5}{\sqrt{30625 + 900}} \approx 0.01408\ \text{m}$.
3. **Peak.** $\Omega_{res} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2} = \sqrt{398} \approx 19.95\ \text{rad/s}$; $A_{max} = \dfrac{F_0/m}{2\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}} = \dfrac{2.5}{2\sqrt{399}} \approx 0.0626\ \text{m}$, about 10 times the static response $F_0/k = 6.25\ \text{mm}$ (≈ Q = 10).
4. **Phase.** $\tan\delta = \dfrac{2\gamma\Omega}{\omega_0^2-\Omega^2} = 30/175 \Rightarrow \delta \approx 0.170\ \text{rad}$: below resonance the response is almost in phase.

</div>

<Layer kind="errori" title="Common mistakes">

<div data-note-id="teoria/risonanza/errori">

- Saying that in steady state the system oscillates at ω₀: it oscillates at the driving angular frequency Ω.
- Confusing the amplitude peak (at √(ω₀²−2γ²)) with the peak of absorbed power (exactly at ω₀).
- Forgetting the transient: right after the driving force is switched on, the motion is the sum of the two solutions.
- Believing that at resonance force and displacement are in phase: they are π/2 out of phase.

</div>
